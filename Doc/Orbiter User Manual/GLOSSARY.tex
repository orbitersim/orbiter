\section{Glossary}
A list of technical terms and abbreviations frequently used in this manual and
elsewhere in Orbiter.

%\begin{table}[H]
	%\centering
	\footnotesize
	\begin{longtable}{ >{\RaggedRight}p{0.22\textwidth} p{0.72\textwidth} }
	\hline%
	Albedo & Fraction of incident radiation reflected back diffusely by a body.\\
	\hline
	Apoapsis & The point of an elliptic orbit farthest from the focus (from the
		orbited body). Body-specific names are sometimes used, e.g.
		\emph{apogee} (for Earth orbits) or \emph{aphelion} (for solar orbits).
		Orbital velocity is lowest at apoapsis. See also \emph{periapsis}.\\
	\hline
	Argument of periapsis & The angle from \textit{ascending node} to
		\emph{periapsis} of an orbit, measured in the direction of motion
		($\omega$). One of the 6 primary 2-body orbital elements (defines the
		orientation of the orbit in the orbital plane).\\
	\hline
	Ascending node & One of the points (nodes) of an orbit intersecting an
		inclined reference plane (e.g. the equator). At the ascending node, the
		orbiting object crosses the reference plane from south to north. See
		also \emph{descending node}.\\
	\hline
	Astronomical unit & One astronomical unit (1\,AU) is the mean radius of
		Earth's orbit around the sun (approximately 150 $\cdot$
		10\textsup{6}\,km). It is commonly used as a distance measure on
		interplanetary scales within the solar system.\\
	\hline
	Axial precession & A change in the orientation of the rotation axis of a
		rotating body (e.g. a planet). For example, Earth's axis is precessing
		around the ecliptic pole at an angle of 23.5\deg{} over a period of
		26000 years.\\
	\hline
	Delta-V & $\Delta v$: The magnitude of a velocity change, specifically, a
		change of a spacecraft's velocity, effected by engaging its engines to
		alter its trajectory. The amount of available $\Delta v$ (\emph{delta-v
		budget}) is limited by the remaining propellant (via the \emph{rocket
		equation}). Fuel is a limited resource, meaning that $\Delta v$
		requirements are an important aspect of mission planning.\\
	\hline
	Descending node & One of the points (nodes) of an orbit intersecting an
		inclined reference plane (e.g. the equator). At the descending node, the
		orbiting object crosses the reference plane from north to south. See
		also \emph{ascending node}.\\
	\hline
	Eccentricity & Shape parameter for conic sections, e.g. 2-body orbits ($e$).
		Circular: $e = 0$, elliptic: $0 < e < 1$, parabolic: $e = 1$,
		hyperbolic: $e > 1$. One of the 6 primary 2-body orbital elements.\\
	\hline
	Ecliptic & The ecliptic is the Earth's mean orbital plane. The orbital
		planes of most planetary bodies are close to the ecliptic, which makes
		it an important reference plane for interplanetary missions. The
		ecliptic undergoes slight variations over time due to gravitational
		perturbations, so a reference date (epoch), e.g. J2000.0 must be added
		to define a fixed plane.\\
	\hline
	Geostationary orbit & A circular equatorial orbit with an orbit period equal
		to the orbited body's sidereal rotation period. As a result, the
		orbiting object maintains its position above a point on the equator,
		at zero ground speed. For Earth, geostationary orbits are at an altitude
		of $\sim$36000\,km.
		Geostationary orbits are often used by communication or weather
		satellites.\\
	\hline
	HUD & Head-up display. Avionics display used in modern aircraft and
		spacecraft that projects data and visual cues onto a transparent screen
		directly in the pilot's line of sight.\\
	\hline
	IDS & Instrument docking system. A fictional universal docking system that
		provides linear and rotational alignment cues for docking approaches,
		similar to ILS.\\
	\hline
	ILS & Instrument Landing System. A combination of radio transmitters
		installed at a runway, and onboard receivers and avionics displays to
		provide an aircraft pilot with deviation cues from a target approach
		path. In Orbiter, this is also used by horizontally landing spacecraft,
		including a (fictional) ILS at the Kennedy SLF.\\
	\hline
	Inclination & The angle between the orbital plane and a reference plane
		($i$). One of the 6 primary orbital elements to describe a 2-body orbit
		(defines the orientation of the orbital plane in space together with
		the \emph{longitude of the ascending node}).\\
	\hline
	Inertia tensor & A 3\,$\times$\,3 matrix that defines a rigid body's
		resistance to a change of its angular velocity around its axes,
		analogous to the role of mass in linear kinematics: $L = I\omega$ ($L$:
		angular momentum, $I$: inertia tensor, $\omega$: angular velocity)\\
	\hline
	Isp & Specific impulse ($I_\mathrm{sp}$). Equivalent to effective exhaust
		velocity $v_{e}$ [m/s]. Specifies the amount of thrust [N] produced by
		burning propellant at a rate of 1\,kg/s. An often used alternative
		definition for $I_{sp}$ is $v_{e} \cdot g_{0}$ [s] with standard gravity
		$g_{0}$ = 9.81\,m/s\textsup{2}.\\
	\hline
	LEO & Low Earth orbit. An orbit with an an apogee altitude of less than
		$\sim$2000\,km and an orbital period of less than $\sim$128 minutes.
		Objects in LEO can experience orbit degradation due to atmospheric
		friction, limiting their lifetime unless periodically boosted to
		compensate, such as the ISS.\\
	\hline
	Line of nodes & The intersection of the orbital plane with an inclined
		reference plane. Contains the \emph{ascending} and \emph{descending
		nodes}.\\
	\hline
	Longitude of the ascending node & The angle in the reference plane between
		reference direction (e.g. vernal point) and the orbit's ascending node
		($\Omega$). One of the primary 2-body orbital elements (defines the
		orientation of the orbital plane in space together with
		\emph{inclination}).\\
	\hline
	Mean anomaly & Fraction of orbital period passed since apoapsis passage,
		expressed as an angle ($M$). Equivalent to true anomaly of a
		hypothetical circular orbit with the same orbital period as the actual
		orbit. Unlike true anomaly in a non-circular orbit, mean anomaly varies
		linearly with time.\\
	\hline
	Mean longitude & The sum of \emph{longitude of the ascending node},
		\emph{argument of periapsis} and \emph{mean anomaly}:
		$l = \Omega + \omega + M$. See also \emph{true longitude}.\\
	\hline
	MET & Mission elapsed time. A time scale for mission events referenced to
		launch time. \\
	\hline
	MFD & Multifunctional display. An avionics instrument consisting of a
		digital display and input device. Can display a variety of data and
		combines multiple traditional aircraft/spacecraft instruments.\\
	\hline
	MJD & Modified Julian Date. A linear time scale counting the number of days
		(and fractional days) since midnight November 17, 1858. Related to the
		Julian Date (JD) by MJD = JD - 2,400,000.5.\\
	\hline
	Normal & The direction perpendicular to a plane. In orbital operations,
		attitudes normal to the orbital plane are often used for out-of-plane
		manoeuvres, such as plane rotations. Orbiter differentiates between
		normal (in the direction of the momentum vector
		$\vec{r} \times \vec{v}$) and anti-normal (in the opposite direction).
		See also \emph{specific relative angular momentum}.\\
	\hline
	Orbital elements & 6 scalar values defining the Keplerian (2-body) orbital
		motion of an object in the unperturbed field of a gravitational point
		source. Shape and size of the orbit are defined by semi-major axis ($a$)
		and eccentricity ($e$); orientation of the orbital plane is defined by
		inclination ($i$) and longitude of the ascending node ($\Omega$);
		orientation of the orbit in the plane is defined by argument of
		periapsis ($\omega$); position of the orbiting object along the orbit is
		given by true anomaly ($\nu$) at a given time. See also \emph{state
		vectors}.\\
	\hline
	Osculating elements & The momentary \emph{orbital elements} of a body
		corresponding to its current \emph{state vectors}. In the presence of
		gravitational forces in addition to the primary, the orbit is perturbed
		and the osculating elements change over time.\\
	\hline
	Patched conic approximation & Approximate trajectory calculation in the
		presence of multiple gravitational sources. The trajectory is
		constructed from partial conic sections, assuming only a single source
		for each section within the \textit{sphere of influence} of that body.
		The sections are joined by continuity conditions for the state
		vectors.\\
	\hline
	Periapsis & The point of an elliptic orbit closest to the focus (the orbited
		body). Body-specific names are sometimes used, e.g. \emph{perigee} (for
		Earth orbits) or \emph{perihelion} (for solar orbits). Orbital velocity
		is highest at periapsis. See also \emph{apoapsis}.\\
	\hline
	PMI & Principal moments of inertia: the diagonal elements of the
		\emph{inertia tensor} expressed in the principal body frame where the
		inertia tensor is diagonal. See "Inertia calculations for composite
		vessels" in Orbiter Technical Reference for more details.\\
	\hline
	Prograde & The \emph{prograde direction} of an orbiting body is that of the
		current orbital velocity vector. In a \emph{prograde orbit} the orbiting
		body is moving in the direction of the rotation of the orbited body. See
		also \emph{retrograde}.\\
	\hline
	RCS & Reaction Control System. A set of thrusters on a spacecraft that
		provides attitude control. Usually engaged in pairs to create angular
		moments (in angular mode) or linear moments (in linear mode) in the 3
		main spacecraft axes.\\
	\hline
	Retrograde & The \textit{retrograde direction} of an orbiting body is the
		direction opposite to the current orbital velocity vector. In a
		\emph{retrograde orbit} the orbiting body is moving in the opposite
		direction to the rotation of the orbited body. See also
		\emph{prograde}.\\
	\hline
	Semi-major axis & Longest half-axis of an ellipse ($a$). One of the 6
		primary 2-body orbital elements (defines the shape of the orbit together
		with the \emph{eccentricity}).\\
	\hline
	Semi-minor axis & Shortest half-axis of an ellipse.\\
	\hline
	Sidereal day & Time for one full (360\deg{}) rotation of a celestial body.
		Used in astronomy, where a star will appear at the same position in the
		sky at the same sidereal time. Shorter than a \emph{synodic day}, which
		also has to take into account the apparent motion of the sun in front of
		the celestial background, caused by the planet's orbital motion.\\
	\hline
	SLF & The Shuttle Landing Facility at the Kennedy Space Center provides one of
		the longest (4600\,m) and widest (91\,m) runways in the world. It was used
		to land the Space Shuttle until 2011. Later uses include X-37B landings and
		commercial enterprises.\\
	\hline
	SOI & Sphere of influence: region around a planet or moon where the
		gravitational influence on an object is predominantly caused by that
		body. Used for approximate trajectory calculations (\emph{patched conic
		approximation}), where the SOI surfaces define the endpoints of the
		conic sections.\\
	\hline
	Specific relative angular momentum & The vector product of an orbiting
		object's position and velocity vectors:
		$\vec{h} = \vec{r} \times \vec{v}$. $\vec{h}$ is normal (perpendicular)
		to the orbital plane.\\
	\hline
	SRB & Solid rocket booster. Solid propellant rocket used to provide
		additional thrust during first stage ascent of a launch vehicle, in
		particular the Space Shuttle.\\
	\hline
	State vectors & Cartesian position ($\vec{r}$) and velocity ($\vec{v}$)
		vectors of an orbiting body at a given time. Uniquely defines an
		unperturbed (Keplerian) orbit. From the state vectors, the 6 orbital
		elements of the corresponding 2-body orbit can be calculated, and vice
		versa.\\
	\hline
	Synodic day & Mean period between two meridian passages of the sun, averaged
		over a year. For a planet rotating prograde, this requires a rotation >
		360\deg{} to account for the orbital movement of the planet around its
		sun, and is therefore longer than a \emph{sidereal day}. The length of a
		synodic day varies annually on a planet with an elliptic orbit.\\
	\hline
	True anomaly & Angular distance of an orbital position from periapsis, as
		measured from the focus ($\nu$).\\
	\hline
	True longitude & The sum of \emph{longitude of the ascending node},
		\emph{argument of periapsis} and \emph{true anomaly}:
		$L = \Omega + \omega + \nu$). See also \emph{mean longitude}.\\
	\hline
	VTOL & Vertical take-off and landing. Spacecraft can use downward directed
		engines to compensate for gravitational acceleration and enable vertical
		take-off and soft touchdown, either using main engines ("tailsitters")
		or dedicated hover engines. Spacecraft with lifting bodies or airfoils
		may more efficiently use horizontal runway take-offs and/or landings on
		planets with sufficient atmospheric density.\\
	\hline
	\end{longtable}
	\normalsize
